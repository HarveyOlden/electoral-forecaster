How the forecast works

Kernelized Electoral Manifold Information Transformer (KERMIT) is a machine-learning model built to forecast the results of US elections. It receives a multiplicity of data, from polls to campaign-finance information, to predict the outcomes of presidential, Senate, gubernatorial and congressional races. The model accesses all of this data simultaneously and, through a transformer architecture, learns how to apply the data at its disposal to each individual election within a cycle. For instance, the model may use the president's approval rating in conjunction with polling in neighbouring districts to predict the outcome of a congressional election that has little information of its own.

Geographic units, be they states, counties or districts, are all represented as tokens. To allow the model to leverage geographic relations to its advantage, attention biases are used to kernelize the relationship between geographic units. In plain terms, when examining the relationship between two geographic units the model is told whether two counties neighbour each other, or whether a given county sits within a given state. Each geographic unit contains the information relevant to the race occurring within that area, represented as a set of rows, and each unit of data itself comes with several encoded features. Because data for this problem is so sparse, synthetic data was used to pretrain the model and ensure it acquires the baseline capabilities required to learn anything of significance from the available data. Synthetic data was generated by selecting an electoral outcome in advance, then modelling subsequent features based on this outcome. Suppose the incumbent president's party lost the election by a wide margin: presidential approval ratings would be drawn from a distribution weighted towards the lower end. Following synthetic pretraining, the model was fine-tuned on actual electoral data. It was then blended with a simpler Bayesian model (with the blend weighted by model confidence), as this was found to successfully decorrelate some errors.
